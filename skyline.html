Skyline Robotics

Fleet Monitoring Platform

B2B SaaS platform for robotic window-cleaning operations

Skyline Robotics builds autonomous robots that clean the windows of skyscrapers. The robots collected a lot of operational data, but the company had no product that let its clients use it.

As the only designer, I designed that product from the ground up: a B2B SaaS platform and its design system, built for three users with different needs. Cleaning company owners track what the fleet saves them, area managers follow every building in real time, and building managers get performance reports.

The challenge

Clients had no way to see what their robots were doing

The robots cleaned buildings across the city, but the people paying for them couldn’t see that from the office. They had no overview of the fleet, couldn’t tell when a cleaning fell behind schedule, and had no data to show the robots were worth it compared with manual cleaning.

Pain points

  • No big-picture view of fleet activity, leading to total reliance on verbal reports.
  • No way to detect recurring issues or deviations from the cleaning schedule.
  • No data-driven evidence of the robots’ efficiency compared to manual labor.
  • Critical business data was trapped in engineering logs, out of reach for decision-makers.

The solution

A hierarchical platform: strategic oversight on top, direct field control below

One platform where owners and managers see the same live data. It answers three questions: Is the fleet working? Are there recurring issues? And is the investment paying off? It also gives managers the real-time control to keep daily operations on schedule.

Key features

  • Live fleet status: every site on one screen.
  • Trends: issues that keep coming back, shown over time.
  • Savings: what the robots save compared with manual cleaning.
  • Drill-down: from the whole fleet down to a single window.

Success criteria

  • Self-serve insights

    Owners and managers find what they need without asking anyone.

  • Reduced support overhead

    Routine status questions answered by the platform, not by the engineering team.

  • Lower operational risk

    Deviations and weather risks are visible early, before they turn into missed cleanings.

Key design decisions

Three decisions that shaped the platform

01

One hierarchy, two entry points

Problem: Owners and area managers look at the same data for different reasons. An owner asks “Is the fleet working, and is it worth it?” An area manager asks “What’s happening in my buildings right now?”

Decision: I designed a drill-down hierarchy (fleet → region → building → window) with a different entry point for each persona. Owners land on the fleet overview, area managers on their region.

Why: Owners need the big picture and only drill down when something goes wrong. Managers start where their daily work is. Neither has to dig through a view built for the other.

Shown in: Global Fleet Overview → Building Deep-Dive

02

Progress you can see on the building

Problem: Area managers need to know exactly where the robot is working and what’s left. Most of them are field people who spend the day outside, not analysts.

Decision: Instead of a percentage or a progress bar, I showed progress as a live grid of the building’s windows, facade by facade, with each window’s status.

Why: A percentage tells you how much is done; the grid shows exactly which windows. For field people, a visual they can read at a glance is easier than numbers.

Shown in: Building Deep-Dive

03

Show the reason, not just the status

Problem: The building list only said Warning or Offline. To understand what happened, the area manager had to open each building.

Decision: Buildings that need attention come first, and each one says what happened and where things stand: an open window, low water pressure, no signal for 7 hours. The map pins show the same thing.

Why: The manager sees what to deal with first at a glance, without opening building after building.

Shown in: Regional Drill-Down

Information architecture

Defining the system flows

To serve both the company owner’s big-picture needs and the area manager’s operational detail, I designed a four-level drill-down: from a Global Fleet Overview down to a single building’s window-level status, and on to its analytics. Each level shows only what’s needed there, and data that used to sit in engineering logs became something managers could act on.

Level 1 · Entry point

Global Fleet Overview

The owner’s entry point answers one question first: is anything wrong right now? What needs attention sits above the areas, and the areas are sorted by priority.

  • KPI cards

    Savings, robots online, cycles on time and robot utilization, each with its trend.

  • Needs your attention

    Buildings at risk of missing a customer deadline, with the reason and a link to the building.

  • Areas by priority

    Critical first, with a color edge, buildings on schedule, robots online and live weather per area.

Level 2 · Regional

Regional Drill-Down (NYC)

The area manager’s entry point: buildings that need attention come first, each with the reason, next to a map with the same statuses.

  • Reason, not just status

    Each warning says what happened, like an open window or low water pressure.

  • Summary bar

    Buildings, cleaning now, needs attention and robots online, at a glance.

  • Weather widget

    Temperature and wind, right next to the buildings.

Level 3 · Building detail

Building Deep-Dive

Window-by-window progress for a single building, with overall progress, an estimated finish date and a cleaning log that records every day of the cycle, including weather stops.

  • Facade tabs

    North, east, south and west, each with its own progress.

  • Window grid

    The facade as a grid, with the live cleaning status of every window.

  • Today

    Drops done, in progress and next, and whether the day is on track.

Building setup

Building Onboarding

Every window in the view above has to be mapped before a robot can start. I designed the screen where the operator enters it on-site, with no developer in between: one drop, duplicated across the facade, with soft checks that catch typos before they reach the robot.

Level 4 · Analytics

Analytics for the fleet or a single building

One analytics screen with a switch at the top. All buildings shows the business view an owner needs. A single building shows why it’s behind.

  • Business view

    Savings, robot utilization, cycles on time and cost per 1,000 sqft, each with its trend.

  • Buildings to look at

    The five buildings most out of range, and why, one click from their analytics.

  • Building view

    Progress against the plan with a forecast finish date, daily pace and the robot’s service status.

Level 4 · Reports

Operational analytics

Performance scores, completed drops and water consumption per building, for client and sustainability reporting.

  • Performance scores

    Actual against expected performance, per building.

  • Completed drops

    Historical data on completed operations.

  • Water consumption

    Usage per day, with anomaly alerts for unusual use.

Results

Impact and outcomes

  • Self-serve insights

    Self-serve

    Customers could check fleet and building status themselves, without asking the engineering team.

  • Support load

    Fewer requests

    Routine “what’s happening with the robot?” questions moved from engineering to the dashboard.

  • Shared view

    One platform

    Owners and area managers worked from the same live data, each starting at their own level.

Key learnings

  • Visibility first

    Showing “what is happening now” before historical analysis matched what users opened the platform for.

  • Operational context

    Putting weather and wind next to the buildings let managers decide on weather stops without switching tools.

  • Dual persona design

    Serving both operational detail (area managers) and an executive overview (company owners) needed a flexible drill-down architecture.

  • System standardization

    Building a design system early let me add new data views quickly while the product was still an MVP.
