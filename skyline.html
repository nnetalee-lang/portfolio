Skyline Robotics

Fleet Monitoring Platform

B2B SaaS platform for robotic window-cleaning operations

Skyline Robotics builds autonomous robots that clean the windows of skyscrapers. The robots collected a lot of operational data, but the company had no product that let its clients use it.

As the only designer, I designed that product from the ground up: a B2B SaaS platform and its design system, built for three users with different needs. Cleaning company owners track what the fleet saves them, area managers follow every building in real time, and building managers get performance reports.

The challenge

Without an interface, stakeholders were blind to operations

While robots cleaned buildings across the city, stakeholders remained operationally blind in the office. Without a high-level view of the fleet, they couldn’t detect performance deviations or provide the proof of work needed to justify robotics over manual labor.

Pain points

  • No big-picture view of fleet activity, leading to total reliance on verbal reports.
  • No way to detect recurring issues or deviations from the cleaning schedule.
  • No data-driven evidence of the robots’ efficiency compared to manual labor.
  • Critical business data was trapped in engineering logs, out of reach for decision-makers.

The solution

A hierarchical platform: strategic oversight on top, direct field control below

A unified platform that gives stakeholders a single source of truth. It answers the three most critical questions: Is the fleet working? Are there recurring issues? And is the investment paying off? It also gives managers the real-time control to keep daily operations on schedule.

Key features

  • Real-time fleet status: instant visibility across all sites.
  • Recurring trend analysis: identifying systematic issues before they escalate.
  • Automated value metrics: live tracking of savings and operational efficiency.
  • Deep-dive hierarchy: a seamless move from the city view to a single robot.

Success criteria

  • Self-serve insights

    Owners and managers find what they need without asking anyone.

  • Reduced support overhead

    Routine status questions answered by the platform, not by the engineering team.

  • Lower operational risk

    Deviations and weather risks are visible early, before they turn into missed cleanings.

Key design decisions

Three decisions that shaped the platform

01

One hierarchy, two entry points

Problem: Owners and area managers look at the same data for different reasons. An owner asks “Is the fleet working, and is it worth it?” An area manager asks “What’s happening in my buildings right now?”

Decision: I designed a drill-down hierarchy (fleet → region → building → window) with a different entry point for each persona. Owners land on the fleet overview, area managers on their region.

Why: Owners need the big picture and only drill down when something goes wrong. Managers start where their daily work is. Neither has to dig through a view built for the other.

Shown in: Global Fleet Overview → Building Deep-Dive

02

Progress you can see on the building

Problem: Area managers need to know exactly where the robot is working and what’s left. Most of them are field people who spend the day outside, not analysts.

Decision: Instead of a percentage or a progress bar, I showed progress as a live grid of the building’s windows, facade by facade, with each window’s status.

Why: A percentage tells you how much is done; the grid shows exactly which windows. For field people, a visual they can read at a glance is easier than numbers.

Shown in: Building Deep-Dive

03

Weather where the decision happens

Problem: Wind and weather decide whether robots can operate safely, and checking them meant switching to another app.

Decision: I placed live weather and wind data next to the cleaning status, and flagged weather stops directly in the cleaning log (“Heavy Winds”).

Why: When a cleaning is paused, the reason is right there, so managers understand it without leaving the platform.

Shown in: Regional Drill-Down and Building Deep-Dive

Information architecture

Defining the system flows

To serve both the company owner’s big-picture needs and the area manager’s operational detail, I designed a four-level drill-down: from a Global Fleet Overview down to a single building’s window-level status, and on to its analytics. Data is never overwhelming, and fragmented engineering logs became one actionable tool.

Results

Impact and outcomes

  • Self-serve insights

    Self-serve

    Customers could check fleet and building status themselves, without asking the engineering team.

  • Support load

    Fewer requests

    Routine “what’s happening with the robot?” questions moved from engineering to the dashboard.

  • Shared view

    One platform

    Owners and area managers worked from the same live data, each starting at their own level.

Key learnings

  • Visibility first

    Prioritizing “what is happening now” over historical analysis was the key to adoption.

  • Operational context

    Putting weather and wind data on the map let managers decide on weather stops without switching tools.

  • Dual persona design

    Serving both operational detail (area managers) and an executive overview (company owners) needed a flexible drill-down architecture.

  • System standardization

    A modular design system let the platform grow its data architecture even while the product was in rapid MVP stages.
